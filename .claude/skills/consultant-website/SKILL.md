# Consultant & Agency Website

Collects the brief for a knowledge or expertise business, then hands off to `website-launch` for the build and deployment.

**Positioning is the hard part here, not the build.** Most consultant websites fail because the visitor cannot tell what is being sold or who it is for. Spend the interview on that.

## How to run this

1. Interview the user with the questions below — use `AskUserQuestion` for the discrete choices.
2. Confirm the brief back in a short summary, **including the positioning statement** — get explicit agreement on it before building.
3. Invoke `website-launch` and follow its workflow from Step 2 (design system) onward.

## Intake questions

**Required before building:**

1. Name — personal brand or company name
2. **Who exactly is the client?** Push past "small businesses" to something specific. Vague answers here produce a website nobody responds to.
3. **What specific problem do they solve?** In the client's words, not industry jargon.
4. **What is the actual offer?** A retainer, a fixed-scope project, an hourly rate, a course, a coaching programme?
5. Price or price range — published, from-price, or on application
6. Proof — case studies, results, notable clients, credentials
7. Primary CTA — discovery call, enquiry form, or direct purchase?
8. Contact details and relevant social profiles
9. Domain — owned or needs buying?

**Ask if relevant:**

10. Do they have permission to name past clients? Many contracts prohibit it.
11. Lead magnet — a guide, template, or audit worth an email address
12. Do they publish content (newsletter, podcast, videos) that should feed the site?
13. Personal brand or company brand — determines whether photography is of them or abstract

## What actually converts

1. **Specificity beats polish.** "I help SaaS companies reduce churn" outperforms "strategic growth partner for ambitious brands" every time. Generic positioning is the number one killer in this category, and clients resist narrowing because it feels like turning down work. Push back: a narrow site generates more enquiries, not fewer.
2. **The visitor must understand the offer within five seconds** of landing. State it plainly in the hero, in their language.
3. **Proof over promises.** One detailed case study with real numbers outperforms ten vague claims of excellence. If they have no case studies, that is the first thing to fix — before the website.
4. **One primary CTA.** Usually a booked call. Multiple competing CTAs across the page reduce total conversions rather than covering more bases.
5. **Show the person.** Buyers are hiring a human. A real photo and a real voice outperform a faceless corporate presentation, even for agencies.
6. **Explain the process.** "What happens after I enquire?" removes the fear of an awkward sales call. A simple three-step process section measurably lifts booking rates.
7. **Email list over social following.** A lead magnet plus an email sequence is usually the highest-return addition to a consultant site — mention it, since most clients under-invest here.

## Page structure

**Home** — hero with a specific positioning statement and the primary CTA · the problem they solve · services/offer · proof (case studies, logos, results) · about the person or team · process explained · testimonials · pricing or how engagement works · FAQ handling objections · closing CTA

**Other pages** — Services (one page per offer if distinct) · Case studies · About · Contact / book a call · Blog or insights · Lead magnet landing page

## Industry rules

- **Never invent case studies, client names, results, or testimonials.** Fabricated results are both a credibility risk and, where they influence a purchase, a consumer protection issue.
- **Client names and logos need permission.** Many consulting contracts include confidentiality clauses that prohibit disclosure. Always ask; never assume a past engagement can be named.
- **Results claims need context.** "Increased revenue 300%" requires the timeframe and circumstances, and ideally a note that results vary.
- **Coaching in regulated areas** — financial, health, legal, or psychological — carries real restrictions. Financial coaching in particular may require licensing depending on jurisdiction, and health or therapy coaching must not imply clinical treatment. Ask what the client is qualified to claim.
- **Earnings claims** ("make $10k a month") are heavily scrutinised by regulators in several jurisdictions and require substantiation and disclaimers. Advise against them.
- **Never invent credentials, qualifications, or years of experience.**

## Design direction

Confident and clean. This category has more freedom than the regulated ones, so let the positioning drive the aesthetic — a corporate strategy consultant and a creative coach should look nothing alike.

The site is often the entire proof of competence, especially for design, marketing, and brand consultants: if the site looks mediocre, the offer is not believable. For those clients, hold a higher visual bar than usual.

Typography does most of the work here. These sites are text-heavy and light on imagery, so a strong type hierarchy, comfortable reading measure, and generous whitespace matter more than decoration. Keep the palette tight.

Avoid stock photos of handshakes, boardrooms, and rising graphs — they signal a generic offer, which is the exact impression to avoid.
